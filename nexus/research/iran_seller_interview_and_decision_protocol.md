# Seller discovery experiment: digital business continuity

Stage: customer discovery. No customer demand or willingness to pay is established.

## Recruit a narrow sample

Target 5–8 owner-operated sellers who currently receive orders through Instagram, Telegram, WhatsApp, Bale, phone calls, or a combination. Start with one segment (for example, home-product sellers) rather than mixing unrelated businesses.

Recruit through personal introductions or public business contact channels. Explain that this is research, not a sales pitch. Do not scrape private groups, send bulk messages, or collect unnecessary personal information.

## 10–15 minute interview script

Opening: “I’m studying how small online sellers keep track of orders when the internet or their usual app is unavailable. I’m not selling anything. You can skip any question.”

1. Think about the most recent time your internet or sales app stopped working. What were you trying to do?
2. Which exact steps stopped working? Which still worked?
3. Did you lose an order, delay a delivery, repeat work, or have a customer complain? What happened?
4. How did you keep track of new and existing orders during that period?
5. Show me the process with dummy information if you are comfortable. Please do not show real customer names, phone numbers, addresses, or payment details.
6. What do you use on a normal day: notebook, Excel, phone notes, a store builder, an assistant, or something else?
7. What do you dislike about that workaround? What works well?
8. How often has this happened in the last three months? What was the practical cost in time, missed orders, or rework?
9. Have you paid for any tool or person to solve this? What did you pay for, and what result did you get?
10. Would you be willing to test a local-only order list using fake data for a few days? What would make it useful or useless?

## Avoid leading questions

Avoid “Wouldn't an offline app solve this?” or “Would you pay for this?” before understanding the current workflow. If asked about price, first establish what they already spend and what measurable value they receive. Interest or a verbal promise is not payment evidence.

## Observation sheet

For each interview, record only:
- Anonymous respondent code (S01, S02, ...).
- Seller segment and approximate order-volume band (optional).
- Channels used (categories only).
- Last outage/reliability incident (respondent-reported; date may be approximate).
- Exact task interrupted.
- Existing workaround.
- Observable friction or time cost (respondent-reported, not independently verified).
- Existing paid tools/services (name and publicly known price only if volunteered).
- Prototype task completed / failed.
- Quote only with explicit permission; otherwise paraphrase.
- Evidence class: respondent_report, observed_in_test, or NEXUS_inference.
- Follow-up permission: yes/no.

Do not record customer identities, phone numbers, addresses, order contents, payment screenshots, account credentials, or private messages.

## Prototype test

Use only dummy data. Ask each participant to:
1. Add a test order.
2. Change its status from new to preparing and then sent.
3. Find orders that still need follow-up.
4. Export a backup file.
5. Close and reopen the local page, then confirm the dummy order remains.
6. Explain what they would still need to do manually if the internet were completely unavailable.

Observe completion without coaching. Record time, errors, confusion and requests. A prototype task completed is usability evidence, not willingness-to-pay evidence.

## Decision thresholds (pre-registered)

These are practical screening rules for this small pilot, not statistical proof.

Continue to a second prototype iteration only if:
- At least 4 of 5–8 interviewees describe a specific recent interruption to order capture or follow-up (not just general dislike of internet outages).
- At least 3 show a recurring workaround that is inconvenient, error-prone, or causes measurable rework.
- At least 3 can complete the core dummy-data workflow without assistance after a short explanation.
- No critical privacy or data-loss concern remains unresolved.

Narrow or pivot if:
- Most respondents already solve the issue adequately with paper, notes or spreadsheets.
- The main pain is actually customer communication, payments, delivery logistics, or platform access, not order recording.
- Sellers need multi-device synchronization or team access immediately; those are outside this prototype.
- The product would require storing sensitive customer data before users can see value.

Stop this specific product hypothesis if:
- Fewer than 2 of 5–8 report a recent concrete workflow interruption.
- No one prefers the prototype to their current workaround after trying it.
- The local storage model is unacceptable to target users.
- No safe, lawful path to reach and test with sellers exists.

## Payment evidence ladder

1. “Sounds useful” — weak interest.
2. Requests a follow-up or asks to try it — stronger interest, still not payment.
3. Repeated voluntary use on their own workflow — behavioral evidence.
4. Explicitly asks for a price or proposal — commercial interest, not a sale.
5. A lawful, clearly described paid pilot or deposit actually completed — payment evidence.

Do not collect money during this research stage. Do not promise security, recovery, sync, or uptime features that the prototype does not have.

## Next action

Run 5–8 interviews, summarize patterns without personal data, and update the candidate status only after reviewing the evidence. Keep confidence and willingness-to-pay fields unknown until supported.
